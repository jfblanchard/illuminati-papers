# A Fugitive's Defense: The Story Behind the 1787 *Anhang*

*A plain-language companion to the translation. For the full text, see
`translations/scholarly/anhang_1787_scholarly.md`; for a version that stays
closer to the original German sentence by sentence, see
`translations/literal/anhang_1787_literal.md`. Every claim below that comes
from the document itself is cited by page number so you can go check it
against the translation — this isn't a substitute for the primary source, just
a way into it.*

## A letter from a man on the run

On the 20th of May, 1787, a man sat down in the city of Wetzlar and wrote a
long, careful letter to an old friend. He had good reason to be careful. Seven
months earlier, agents of the Bavarian government had raided his house in
Landshut and carried off his papers. Since then, those papers — his private
correspondence, his notes, his half-finished essays, even things that belonged
to his wife and his brother — had been published by the government itself, in
a book called *Einige Originalschriften des Illuminatenordens* ("Some Original
Writings of the Illuminati Order"), presented to the public as proof of a
dangerous secret conspiracy.

The man writing from Wetzlar was Franz Xaver von Zwack — we know this for
certain because he signs the letter himself, "Zwackh" (p. 35), and because it
comes with an attached government certificate addressed to "Franz Xav. v.
Zwackh," identifying him by the same title the original 1786 raid targeted:
Regierungsrath, a government councillor, at Landshut. He'd fled the country
weeks before the certificate — dated 29 November 1786 — was even issued,
escaping arrest around 14 October, just two days after the search of his
house (p. 36). This document, published the following year as an *Anhang*
("Appendix") to the original collection, is his rebuttal: a point-by-point
takedown of what the government chose to print about him, written to a
former superior in the secret society at the center of the whole affair — the
Bavarian Illuminati.

It's a strange document to read cold. It jumps between genuine legal argument,
wounded pride, dry sarcasm, and real personal pain, often within the same
paragraph. Someone else — probably whoever actually compiled and published
this collection, a sympathizer rather than a hostile editor — occasionally
interrupts with a footnote correcting one of Zwack's specific claims, which
means the document has two voices in conversation with each other, not one.
And it keeps circling back to things that seem bizarre out of context — like a
recipe for poisoning a pig. So here's the story behind it, in order.

## Who was Zwack, and why was his house searched?

Bavaria in the 1780s was ruled by an Elector who had grown convinced — not
without cause — that a secret society called the Illuminati, founded in 1776
by a professor named Adam Weishaupt, had spread through his government,
universities, and clergy. The order used code names instead of members' real
identities (Weishaupt himself was "Spartacus"); Zwack does not use a code name
in this letter, and none of the surviving evidence in this corpus has yet
matched him to one, but his role as a senior figure — addressed here as a
former superior's peer and clearly trusted with the order's internal
paperwork — is not in doubt. In October 1786, the government sent officials to
search his house in Landshut. They found a large personal archive: letters,
essays, drafts, half-finished ideas, and — as you'll see — quite a lot of
material that had nothing to do with the Illuminati at all, just swept up
because it happened to be in the same drawers and cabinets.

The government published a selection of what it found the following year,
framed as damning evidence. Zwack's *Anhang* is his answer to that selection,
written from exile, addressed to someone who could still publish a companion
defense of his own (Zwack refers throughout to his friend's forthcoming
"Apology," p. 4-5).

## "That's not even mine" — the Mannheim gossip sheet

One of the first things Zwack tackles is almost funny, once you see the whole
picture. The government's publication solemnly notes that the house-search
turned up "brief character-sketches of 95 women in Mannheim," written in
French, titled *Portraits des Demoiselles à Mannheim* (p. 10) — presented as
if it were some kind of Illuminati intelligence file.

Zwack's answer: it's a piece of gossip. A "Pasquill" — an anonymous satirical
pamphlet, the 18th-century equivalent of a tabloid — that had circulated in
Mannheim a few years earlier, given to him by a third party, and which ended
up in a cabinet that actually belonged to his *wife*, mixed in with her own
papers. It has nothing to do with the Illuminati. Zwack's real complaint here
isn't really about the pamphlet — it's about method: if the government is
willing to present something this obviously unrelated as sinister evidence,
what does that say about how they built the rest of their case? (p. 10-11)

## The women's club that never happened

A similar story: the government printed an old proposal for founding a
"Weiberorden" — a women's auxiliary order — as though it were a real Illuminati
institution. Zwack's response is that this was a rough, disorganized personal
essay he scribbled years earlier "in an idle hour," borrowing the idea loosely
from a real historical group called the **Mopsorden** (the "Order of the
Pug"), an actual mid-18th-century society that, unlike the Freemasons, did
admit women. He says he rejected the idea almost immediately and never
proposed it to anyone — a claim he says every senior member of the order can
confirm (p. 9). Whether that's true is impossible to verify from this
document alone; it's presented here as his defense, not as settled fact.

## Church money, moneylenders, and a man named Pylades

The longest and most detailed section of the letter concerns money — and it's
worth walking through carefully because it's the closest thing in the document
to a real, checkable financial scandal (or the rebuttal of one).

The government's publication claimed that, through Illuminati influence, a
member known as **Pylades** became a kind of in-house legal officer for a
Bavarian church council, giving the order effective control over church funds
(p. 15). Zwack's rebuttal is detailed and specific: those funds had *always*
been lent out, mostly to the nobility, as a completely normal practice; any
subject could borrow against them once proper security was arranged; and
Pylades's actual job was narrow — vetting the financial security behind loan
requests before passing them to a council of twelve, where he was one of only
two Illuminati members. Zwack's specific example: three brothers, ruined by
loan sharks, got refinanced through this system and freed from their debts
(p. 16-18). By the time this letter was written, Pylades was already dead
(p. 18) — one of several small, sad details buried in the legal argument.

A second footnote-writer — not Zwack himself — jumps in here to flatly deny a
related claim, that four Bavarian church pulpits were held by Illuminati
members: "I know of not a single one," he writes, offering to check the
order's own membership rolls (p. 15). This is one of the moments that reveals
the second voice in the document — someone Illuminati-aligned but independent
enough to correct Zwack's own side when a specific claim looks shaky.

## The smuggling scheme that was never tried

Buried among what the government called dangerous Illuminati schemes is a
short note describing a plan to place a member inside a foreign ambassador's
staff, so goods could move through customs duty-free under diplomatic cover,
with the profits going to the order (p. 19). It reads like something out of a
spy novel — and Zwack's answer punctures it almost immediately: the Illuminati
had no ambassador and nobody in any embassy's service anywhere in Bavaria, so
there was no one who could have carried it out. The idea came from a member
named **Coriolan**, an early, junior member who worked at the state lottery
and was always looking for ways to fund the order; Zwack, as his superior at
the time, personally judged the scheme unworkable and filed it away — he says
his friend won't even remember hearing of it (p. 19-21).

## The pig, the poison, and the professor: Aqua Toffana
pra
The government's publication describes material attributed to a member's
notebook — signed with the code name **Ajax** — including a recipe for
something called **Aqua Toffana**, plus several recipes "for procuring
abortion" (p. 22). This is the darkest material in the whole document, and
Zwack addresses it head-on rather than dodging it.

First, some real history: Aqua Toffana was a genuine, notorious poison,
associated with a woman named Giulia Tofana in 17th-century Italy, allegedly
used by hundreds of women to escape abusive marriages. By the late 1700s its
"real" recipe was more legend than fact — different books offered wildly
different, often absurd versions of what it supposedly contained.

The version quoted in this document is one of those absurd ones: fatten a
young pig on arsenic, then distill its fat. Zwack doesn't defend the recipe —
he mocks it, and says respectable experts already had, publicly, years
earlier: the pig would die from the arsenic before you could finish fattening
it, and the resulting fat would reek so badly that nobody could use it without
being caught immediately (p. 26). He specifically names a real historian,
**Archenholz**, as having already published a more plausible (and presumably
less absurd) account of the poison's actual ingredients.

His broader defense for having *any* of this material — poison recipes,
abortion-related notes, and all — is that none of it was original or secret.
He says he got it, along with a large collection of other scientific curios,
from a colleague named **von Massenhausen**, a real Bavarian court official
who was in prison by the time this letter was written (p. 22-23). And he
points out that the poison chapter specifically was already public: a famous
Jesuit scholar, **Athanasius Kircher**, had published an entire chapter on
poisons and how to prepare them in his own widely available book, the *Mundus
Subterraneus* — cited here in a footnote, probably by the same second voice
that corrected the Pylades claim (p. 23). If a Jesuit could publish this
openly, Zwack argues, how can a private collection of the same, already-public
information be treated as a crime?

Then he says the quiet part directly. Referring to his own position as a
sitting **criminal judge**, he asks: does having a recipe for poison in your
files make you a poisoner? Has he ever actually procured an abortion? He
argues his entire record answers the question (p. 25). It's the most direct,
personal moment of self-defense in the whole letter — and, notably, not one
this project can independently verify. It's Zwack's own account, written to
save his career and possibly his life. Read it as his side of the case, not
as an established fact.

## A confession from a 19-year-old, stolen from a novel

The next accusation is, in its own way, the cruelest. The government's
publication claimed pages of Zwack's seized papers showed he had once
attempted suicide — and, according to Zwack, backed this up with a passage
copied, without credit, straight out of Goethe's *The Sorrows of Young
Werther* (1774), one of the most famous and controversial novels of the
century, notorious even at the time for being linked to real copycat
suicides. Someone had lifted the passage, attributed it to Zwack, and attached
what he calls a "spiteful note" that also, gratuitously, insults a dead young
woman's reputation (p. 28-29).

But behind the accusation is something real: a genuine account, in Zwack's own
words, of a period of suicidal despair when he was nineteen years old — found,
he says, in a private confession document seized in the same search. He
describes it plainly: not really a resolved plan to die, but an outlet during
a period of depression, something he already confessed and felt remorse over
long before any of this became public, and something he says he's grateful he
came through (p. 29-30). Whatever else this document is — a legal defense, a
piece of political theater — this passage reads as a real man describing a
real breakdown, dragged into public print without his consent, years after
the fact. It deserves to be read that way: seriously, not as scandal.

## Ten years of service, repaid with exile

By the closing pages, the tone shifts from rebuttal to something closer to
grief. Zwack points out that he served the Bavarian state loyally for ten
years (p. 30), and that this — his exposed private life, his youthful
mistakes, his confidential papers — is how it was repaid. He forgives his
enemies, tells his friend to take care, and signs off with real warmth,
calling him "my former teacher" even though the order that once bound them
together has been dissolved (p. 34-35).

Then two real documents are attached. The first is a formal character
reference, dated 29 November 1786 — six weeks *after* Zwack had already fled —
signed and sealed by three real Bavarian officials (Melchior, Count von
Preysing; Carl, Baron von Pauli; and Carl Albrecht, Nobleman von Vacchieri),
testifying that he had served with total honesty and diligence and that not
one complaint had ever been raised against him (p. 35-36). A footnote points
out the bitter irony directly: the same man receiving these glowing
references from an entire panel of judges had, two weeks earlier, already had
to run for his life (p. 36).

The second attachment is almost sweet by comparison: a warm, mundane 1786
letter from Baron von Dachsberg, a real Bavarian regional administrator,
about a travel permit and Zwack's career prospects — the kind of ordinary,
friendly correspondence you'd expect between two colleagues who liked each
other, with no hint of the disaster to come (p. 37-39).

## Why this document matters

Taken as a whole, the *Anhang* isn't really a smoking gun for either side of
the Illuminati controversy. It's something more interesting: one specific,
named, real historical person's attempt to answer, in detail, exactly what
was printed about him, while on the run from his own government. Some of what
he says can already be checked against real history — Kircher's book is real,
the Mopsorden was real, Goethe's *Werther* was real, and the officials who
signed his character reference were real people holding real offices. Other
claims — that the poison recipes were never used, that the suicide passage
means nothing more than what he says it means, that he really did nothing
wrong — are his word, made under obvious pressure to clear his name, and
should be weighed as such.

What makes it worth reading isn't a verdict on Zwack's guilt or innocence. It's
that, four hundred pages into a government propaganda campaign against a
secret society, one actual person — with a wife, a brother, a dead colleague,
an old teacher he still loved, and a bad decision he made at nineteen —
briefly steps out from behind the myth.
