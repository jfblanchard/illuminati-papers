# Nachtrag von weitern Originalschriften (1787) — Scholarly Translation

## Methodology & status
Translated by Devin (Sonnet) on 2026-10-15/16, directly from
`transcriptions/nachtrag_1787/nachtrag_1787_corrected.txt` (the Phase 3
corrected German text, PDF pages 5-436), informed throughout by the literal
pass in `translations/literal/nachtrag_1787_literal.md` (which covers
pp.5-340; for pp.341-436, beyond the literal pass's reach, this translation
works directly from the corrected German, cross-checked against
`translations/nachtrag_1787_translation_context.md`). This is the
**readable** pass: natural modern English prose, long subordinate clauses
broken up where English demands it, archaic diction updated — but nothing
added, softened, or summarized away. Genuinely ambiguous passages are
translated on their most literal defensible reading and flagged inline with
`[?]`, never silently smoothed over.

Unlike the Anhang — a single sympathetic defense letter — the Nachtrag is a
prosecution-style compilation: Weishaupt's and his correspondents' own
letters, reprinted in per-correspondent "case files," interleaved with
hostile editorial footnotes that name Weishaupt directly and press
accusations of incest, attempted abortion, corruption of youth, and
subversion of religion and the state. Where the editorial voice breaks in on
a letter, it is set off here as a block quote (as the Anhang pass did for its
footnote-writer), so the two voices stay visually distinct — the footnote
voice is hostile, sarcastic, and third-person about Weishaupt; the letters
themselves are not.

House conventions, matching the literal pass and the translation-context
file:
- **☉** (circle-with-dot) is kept as printed; it stands for "der Orden," the
  Order — never "sun."
- **▢ / ▢▢** is kept as printed; it stands for "Loge"/"Logen," lodge/lodges.
- **Redacted names** (V===, S==, B.==, and similar dash/equals forms) are
  preserved exactly as printed; none are resolved or guessed.
- The **"x."-dot notation** (M.x.x., R.x.) is a Masonic raised-dot
  abbreviation mark; kept as printed.
- **Latin and French** passages are left in the original language, as direct
  quotations forming part of the document's own texture; an English
  rendering follows in brackets on first occurrence of a given passage.
- **Code names** (Spartacus, Philo, Celsus, Cato, Marius, Epictet, Hannibal,
  Diomedes, etc.) are kept as-is, including inconsistent spelling variants
  exactly as the corrected text has them.
- **Letter headers** in the Latin epistolary formula (e.g. "Sp. C. S. d." =
  "Spartacus Celso salutem dicit," Spartacus sends Celsus his greetings) are
  translated to a plain salutation line under each letter's number, glossed
  on first occurrence.
- The letters' formal "ſie" (Sie, the polite "you") is rendered as "you"
  throughout, for readability, rather than literally as "they."
- **Persian-calendar month names** and the "Jezdedgerd" era (Benmeh, Dimeh,
  Meharmeh, Abenmeh, Merded, Thirmeh, Asphandar, Chardad, Pharavardin) are
  kept as printed.

This is an AI-assisted first pass, **not** a final scholarly translation —
pending human review. Page markers (`--- page N ---`, PDF page numbers)
match the corrected German text and the literal pass for citation purposes.

**First Part. CORRESPONDENCE.**
**I.**

Further Original Letters from Spartacus (Weishaupt.)

**1.**
*Spartacus to Celsus. Greetings.* [Latin header in the original: "Sp. C. S.
d." — Spartacus Celso salutem dicit.]

I have now read all the Communicata, and will send them back next Thursday
by the messenger. I can see how much trouble you are taking to bring
everything into order, little by little. But it will always be hard work,
for the corruption is very great, and indifference toward the ☉ has gained
the upper hand considerably.

--- page 10 ---

To bring this about more effectively, I would apply the following measures.

1) I would look about for steady, active, diligent men. I believe (24.) 3==
[?] should not be overlooked. You will read the rest of my plan in my letter
to Celsus.

2) In the assemblies I would put at the head people who have standing and
authority, who know how to set the tone of thinking, who are revered by the
young as oracles, and who are, besides, serious and strict in their morals.

3) I have had the recruiting done by people themselves suited for it, who
also know how to train the people. Plinius and Pythagoras have excellent
skill in this.

4) The overly lukewarm and negligent — people of poor morals and
reputation, or heads simply too damp — I would seek to remove, not by direct
exclusion, but either by heaping work and burdens on them until they
themselves asked for their discharge, or by uniting them all into a separate
assembly under equally negligent superiors, so that it

--- page 11 ---

dies out of its own accord. The somewhat less unfit I would simply neglect
entirely, and stop writing to them.

5) A chief trick is this: the newly admitted must not, from the very first
day, enjoy all the privileges, acquaintance, and company of the veterans.

6) I would assign the young people to a steady, educated man for
instruction.

7) Those put in charge of instructing them should never make themselves too
familiar with their subordinates — let themselves be sought after instead.

8) But above all it depends on whom they admit into the Illuminati class: if
these are well chosen, things can go quite well. I think, though, that they
must bring new blood into the ailing body. If new, previously unknown,
respected, respectable persons appear in this class, the consequences will
be most agreeable.

9) Above all, the goodness of the cause is best recommended by one's own
example. One must be — or at least appear to be — oneself what one wants to
make of others. Nothing harms a good cause

--- page 12 ---

more than when words and deeds fail to match. They must honor and respect
one another, speak well of each other, treat people kindly and
affectionately, and let them see that their welfare is truly a concern.
Imperceptibly, those around them take on their manners, and come to love the
cause for the sake of the person they deal with. Ask Pythagoras sometime how
I treat him — he never leaves me without a felt esteem and a glowing zeal.
My quiet, withdrawn way of life, my disinterested intentions, and my warm
encouragement certainly contribute no small part to this; for he can see
that I feel what I say, that I have not merely learned it by rote but truly
felt it. Oh, example — example achieves more than any words or commands! I
must, before I command another, first make him inclined to await my
commands. Moral government is of a wholly peculiar kind, with its own
peculiar principles, and without it our whole undertaking is a mere chimera,
nothing more than a beautiful dream. Read diligently, and examine
yourselves; put yourselves in others' place; find out what impression you
make on them. By this path I have come as far in morals as perhaps any human
being ever has. Believe me —

--- page 13 ---

believe me, dearest Cato! I write from the heart, and I write to a friend:
what I tell him, I have experienced — often experienced, and found to be
true. And you shall see it, down to the point of conviction, in the
Illuminatus dirigens grade: that I have never, until now, demanded anything
out of mere obstinacy, but only as a genuine means to our purpose. Were I
myself in Athens, with direct authority, you would see how I would go about
it — how, through nothing but manner and example, through two or three
persons on whom I would spend my whole strength, effort, and eloquence, I
would want to set everyone else right, indirectly, through these very same
channels. But why be so long-winded about it — my grade of the Illuminatus
minor already contains all of it. Whoever rightly understands it, makes it
his own, and puts it into practice, will certainly not fail to change men.
We must first make the people, and that takes effort.

Names for your province will follow shortly; I have left the correction of
the geography to Philo.

Why should Alexander not know me under the name Spartacus? Does this name
not serve my purpose — concealing my own —

--- page 14 ---

just as well as any other? I set no pride in splendid names. One must have a
childish soul to prefer being called Caesar rather than Spartacus. But if it
were necessary, I am also called Sauhuniaton [?] — though how would I manage
that with the others, who know me as Spartacus? Farewell, and love me. I am

Your wholly devoted Spartacus.
Ephesus, 27 January 1781.

**2.**
*Spartacus to the Areopagites. Greetings.*

I congratulate you on the new office, and wish that every Areopagite might
become a privy councillor with a salary of 20,000 florins. But I wish even
more that they obtain offices where they do not have much official work to
do.

Here follow the still-outstanding quibus licet. They are, admittedly, not
what they ought to be — sed spiritus non spirabat [but the spirit would not
breathe] — and I have far too much to do. I cannot commit myself to it
again another time. As for the letter to the assembly, I still need some
time; it must come out good and forceful, or else

--- page 15 ---

it will do little good. I doubt I can finish it before the start of the
year, so do not count on it with any certainty.

That you did not understand much of Philo's reports, I readily believe — it
often happens to me too, for I still have no roster of the people there, any
more than I do for Athens.

I have received orders from the ecclesiastical council to propose an
inspector for the local German schools. He will necessarily have to be
salaried as well. I will propose Pythagoras; it suits him. Support this with
Alfred and E⸗ === — a salary could be worked out of the local simple
benefices.

So that Hermes meets no resistance from the university, he must not demand
too high a stipend, for we are not in a position to provide it. I am
thinking 600 florins and the stole fees. Only, he should not push his
pretensions too high at the start, until he has a firm footing — after
that, he must also become a professor.

Epictet, to be sure, has his shortcomings too; but in truth I know of not a
single man in the whole ☉ who is entirely what I wish him to be. Carelessness,
love of dominion, and

--- page 16 ---

short-sightedness are the besetting faults of most of our members; yet I
hope the work itself, and the experience it brings, will shape them — they
are, after all, nearly all still beginners, and very few of them yet grasp
even half of the ideas they truly need. If I had encountered already formed
men, it would of course be easier; but the hardest work of all is that I
must first form the Areopagites to my own purpose, and they do not willingly
follow, since they believe they have as much right as I do, and know just as
much. Hinc illae irae [hence that anger] — though this has changed
considerably of late. They will come to understand, more and more, how much
effort and skill it takes to unite men in a lasting way. The means that
promise the swiftest effect are precisely the most inadequate. Oh, that is a
great art — the greatest of all! Only consider how difficult it must be,
since so many have attempted it, and so few have ever succeeded.

The M.x.x. are no honorary grades: they must be made as useful and
instructive as possible. I will set about reworking the whole system myself,
as soon as I receive the cahiers. Then, à la Jesuit, not a single line
hinting at any design against religion or the state, nothing ambiguous,

--- page 17 ---

shall ever appear — everything prepared hand in hand, nothing without
reason. In the meantime, everything stays as it is. People are told that the
true Masonic system will only appear once the caterpillar has been cleansed
of the impure men who presently infest it. The Minerval and the other grades
were invented precisely to work the worthless members out through labor. If
that takes three or four years, it is soon enough — I will then have each
grade printed and distributed among the lodges [▢▢]. My plan is this:

1. Novitiate. Remains almost entirely as is.

2. Junior and Minerval are merged into a single grade.

3. Lesser Illuminatus and Fellow[-craft]. Likewise merged.

4. Master and Greater Illuminatus, similarly.

5. Illuminatus dirigens and Master-builder/Architect, similarly. The
Scottish Knighthood does not please me.

Tandem Mysteria [at last, the Mysteries] — certainly worth the effort.

The machine must become simple enough that a child could run it and set it
in motion. Hoc nondum est [this is not yet so] — though it would be, had I
not been hindered. The grades must not only contain nothing dangerous or
ambiguous for

--- page 18 ---

us, but must be so arranged that princes themselves beg us to settle in
their lands and establish them there.

But should the Areopagites not approve of my putting the matter on this
footing, we shall not quarrel over it — I will simply work for myself alone,
if only to leave behind the ideal, so that posterity can judge and compare;
or I can just let the matter rest. Favores non debent obtrudi [favors ought
not to be forced on anyone].

As for Edessa, I have hope that Agathocles and his associates will come
around of their own accord, once they see they have knocked in vain at every
door. One cannot possibly give in to them, for they are merely trying to spy
out our secrets: they want nothing but the bare grades, follow nothing in
the least, and laugh and mock at our ceremonies. In short, precisely because
they are rich, they also possess, to a high degree, every fault of the rich
— ignorance, pride, a spirit of independence, and an aversion to work. Leave
this matter to Philo alone. Diomedes need not feel he has compromised
himself, for he admitted them and promised them nothing further than what
their proper statutory conduct would earn — atqui hoc non faciunt [and yet
they do not do this].

--- page 19 ---

We can do entirely without them in any case, for we have people in Edessa
who are worth incomparably more.

Would it not be good to order Socrates to recruit B. W==? I think he would
not be a bad man for it.

Never let yourselves be drawn into proving that you are the genuine
Freemasons. The best proof is no proof at all. Whoever refuses to believe
it may go look elsewhere. The man who proves it best is the one who knows
the secret — and the one I can most readily assume knows it is the one
whose own arrangements suggest something great and serious. Carry this rule,
along with the earlier one about the coming changes, into the Instruction
for Areopagites that was recently sent out.

To keep your own freedom of speech in reserve, let it be noticed now and
then that superiors enjoy great latitude on this point — that they speak one
way today and another tomorrow, and often ask a question with great
confidence simply to draw out their members' answers and modes of thought.
This evasion lets you make good on many mistakes already made. One must
always say that the end will show

--- page 20 ---

which statement was the true one. Speak now one way, now another, so as
never to talk yourself into a corner, and so as to remain impenetrable to
your subordinates as to your true way of thinking. Etiam hoc inseratur
instructioni [let this too be inserted into the instruction]. It is even
better, and even less noticeable, if the Illuminatus major is given the task
of varying his speech with his subordinates. Ex rationibus supra adductis
[for the reasons given above].

I beg you, do not let these maxims — which come up often in my letters — go
to waste: enter them always into the Areopagite instruction, for they do not
always occur to me when I need them. In time, a splendid political grade
could be made out of them; Philo has already been doing this for a long
time. So share these instructions of yours with one another, so that in time
a single one can be formed out of them, and read them diligently, so that
they become second nature to you. Although I know them all myself, and act
on them, I am still not able to set them all down in order at once. By these
maxims, you will also be better able to judge my projects and my way of
operating.

I am told that in Athens the whole city already knows that the ▢ has bought
the house everyone has been talking about. Malum est [that is a bad
thing]. There you are badly exposed to observation — you are playing almost
with your cards face up. One more proof of

--- page 21 ---

the carelessness, the loose talk, and the vain boastfulness of your
subordinates. For my part, I would never have advised buying a house at all,
had they consulted me re adhuc integra [while the matter still stood open].
There is, I believe, no real danger from this exposure, but a great deal of
force is lost by it all the same.

And what, then, is Alfred doing? How is he coming along? I hear nothing at
all from him. What is Hannibal doing? If he is as active in Italy as he is
diligent in writing, he will not get very far. Tiberius does nothing at
all — and I think that for that very reason he should be told nothing
either. One must never let idleness and negligence earn what ought to be a
reward for diligence and loyalty alone, or one only tempts others to do the
same, until in the end everyone grows lazy. If everyone wanted to be as
diligent as Tiberius, what would we have left to write to one another at
all? Or must we work simply so that he has something to read? Such men are
drones, consuming the honey the other worker bees have made. Valete, et
diligite vos invicem, sicut Christus dilexit Ecclesiam [farewell, and love
one another, as Christ loved the Church]. From Erzerum I have received no
provincial report, and so I have had nothing to send him either.

Ephesus, 15 Asphandar 1151.
Spartacus.

Facile cum valemus, aegrotis consilia damus [it is easy, when we are well,
to give advice to the sick].

On the division of the prefectures I agree with you entirely; changes have
also taken place in the other provinces.

Thank heaven, and your kind efforts, that v.— has finally made the proposal.
All the same, it has cost me my honor for fourteen days — though of course
he has no idea the matter is so urgent. Meanwhile the deliverance is
beginning, before long, to come into view. If only Hannibal would do me the
favor of letting her stay for a while at Sandersdorf: I would gladly pay for

**5.**
*Spartacus to Celsus. Greetings.*

I thank you for your continued kind efforts, and beg you to pursue this
matter further, and as forcefully as you can. This is perhaps the only time
I have ever burdened my friends with a request, and I would not do so even
now, if my whole fate — honor, fortune, and misfortune, so to speak — did
not depend on the speediest possible success of it. V=== has certainly done
me great harm through his delay. Is Annibal not coming soon? If not, would
you be so good as to arrange, in Sandersdorff, that I might lodge my
sister-in-law there, at my own expense, for as long as it takes until I know
how matters stand? Annibal, who is a very old acquaintance of mine, will
have nothing against it, especially since I am asking for nothing but the
bare lodging.

Qui fit, ut voluptatem dolor comes sequatur? [How does it happen that pain

Every misfortune strikes me at once. Now my mother, too, has died! A
funeral, a wedding, a christening — all one after another, in so short a
time. What a strange jumble! May I ask you to advance my sister 50 florins
on my account, to cover the funeral costs? I am the only one among you who
has, for a long time, paid every expense for the ☉ out of his own pocket,
setting myself back so badly in my family affairs that I do not even have
enough cash on hand at present to bury my mother. So little have I made use
of the Order! I am not asking that this money

--- page 30 ---

be given to me out of the treasury, but as a loan — repayable, at the very
latest, by Easter, which I think I have every right to expect. Nothing will
be lost by it, for, thank God, I still have hopes of various sums of income;
they simply are not due yet, just when I need them most.

Because of the sorry state of the ☉, I am writing to Diomedes himself again
today; but since I still have household matters to attend to, the letter
cannot go to the post today after all. These are the sad consequences when
a man believes himself too safe and too strong, pushes everything too
loudly and openly, thereby turns others against him, sets prudence aside,
forms cabals too soon, lets his self-interested, domineering intentions
show, abandons cool-headed counsel, and neglects his own proper formation
and improvement. Learn from this, people — you have taken up such a system
far too lightly: you want the end, but you neglect the means. I will write
my remaining thoughts to Diomedes tomorrow. Farewell — happier than I am. I
am

Your Sp.
Ephesus, 12 December 1783.
